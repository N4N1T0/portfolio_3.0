The product page is where an eCommerce store wins or loses the session. Traffic problems get the attention, but a store with decent traffic and a weak product page simply converts worse at the same cost per visit.

These are eight checks I would apply before touching ad spend. Each one is observable without a redesign, and most can be measured with an A/B test or a session recording review.

## 1. One primary action, visible without scrolling

The call to action should state the outcome: "Add to cart", not "Submit". It must be reachable with a thumb on a phone, sized as a real button rather than a text link, and it should stay visible when the page is long.

Two competing primary actions — "Buy now" and "Request a quote" side by side — split intent. Pick one and make the secondary action visibly secondary.

## 2. Photography that answers the questions

Product photography is not decoration; it removes uncertainty. A useful set covers:

- The product alone, on a neutral background.
- Scale, next to something familiar.
- Detail of the part buyers worry about: the seam, the clasp, the sole.
- The product in use, if the use case is not obvious.

Every image needs `alt` text that describes it, plus explicit `width` and `height` so the page does not shift as they load. That is a conversion fix and a [Core Web Vitals](/en/blog/responsive-design-guide/) fix at the same time.

## 3. Variants that update everything

Changing colour should update the image, the SKU, the price if it differs, and the stock message. A variant selector that changes only the swatch forces the buyer to guess whether they are looking at the right product.

If a variant is out of stock, say so on the swatch instead of after the click. The most expensive frustration in eCommerce is finding out at checkout.

## 4. Detail at the depth the buyer needs

There is a level of detail for someone who knows the product and another for someone who does not. The page has to serve both without a wall of text:

- Three to five benefit-led bullets above the fold.
- A specifications table for the people who compare numbers.
- Shipping, returns and sizing in an expandable block, not a separate page.

Expandable sections are fine for secondary detail. They are not fine for information the buyer needs to decide, such as the delivery date.

## 5. Brand consistency where it builds trust

Consistent typography, colour and imagery across listing, product and checkout signals that the same company owns the whole journey. The failure is visible: a product page that looks premium followed by a checkout that looks like a different site.

This is the same principle as [designing a brand system rather than a mood board](/en/blog/graphic-design-digital-marketing/), applied at the point of purchase.

## 6. Copy that sells the outcome, not the feature list

Features describe the product; benefits describe the buyer's life. "420 g" is a feature; "light enough to carry all day" is the same fact doing work.

Keep the voice human and specific. If the copy could belong to any product in the category, it is not helping. Write the first sentence for someone who arrived from a search result and does not know the brand.

## 7. Trust signals next to the decision

Reviews, ratings and return policy belong near the add-to-cart button, not in the footer. A buyer hesitating at the button should not have to scroll to find out whether returns are free.

Baymard's [product page research](https://baymard.com/research/product-page) is the reference I check for what buyers look for at that point.

Two practical rules: show the number of reviews, not only the average, and never hide a negative review entirely. A page with only five-star reviews reads as curated and loses credibility.

## 8. Measure the page, not the campaign

Optimisation without measurement is opinion. Three signals tell you where the page fails:

- **Add-to-cart rate** by product: low means the page or the price, not the traffic.
- **Checkout completion** by device: a gap on mobile is usually a layout or form problem.
- **Scroll depth and session recordings**: where people stop reading is where the page stops answering.

Test one change at a time and let it run long enough to be meaningful. A week is usually too short, and a test stopped at the first good number is a decision, not a result.

## What to fix first

If the page has all eight problems, fix them in this order: the primary action, the images, the variants, then the copy. The first three remove friction; the copy improves persuasion. Friction first is almost always the cheaper win.
