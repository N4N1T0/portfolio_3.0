"Tell a story" is the most repeated and least actionable piece of marketing advice. A website is not a novel: nobody reads it in order, and most visitors arrive on a product page from a search result. The narrative that survives that behaviour is not a story arc. It is a small set of claims, repeated consistently, each one backed by something the page can show.

That is what I build on client sites, and it usually fits on one page.

## One sentence that cannot be swapped

Start with a positioning sentence. Not a slogan, not a value proposition deck, a sentence that would be false for a competitor:

> For independent hotels, we handle direct bookings end to end so the front desk stops losing reservations to commission sites.

The test is substitution: if you can replace your name with a competitor's and the sentence still reads true, it says nothing. A useful sentence names a specific audience, a specific job and a specific outcome.

Everything else on the site is an elaboration of that sentence. If a section cannot be traced back to it, the section is either a different message or filler.

## The homepage is a sequence, not a summary

Visitors decide in seconds, then read if the promise matches. The order matters more than the wording:

1. **Promise.** What changes for the visitor, in their words. One sentence.
2. **Mechanism.** How it works, in three steps or one short paragraph. This is where doubt starts, so answer it before it forms.
3. **Proof.** A number, a client, a screenshot of the real product, a testimonial that names the objection out loud.
4. **Next step.** One action. Two competing calls to action halve the response to both.

A common failure is putting the company history second. "Founded in 2009" answers a question the visitor has not asked yet, and it delays the mechanism, which is what they actually need.

## Proof beats adjectives

"High quality", "passionate" and "innovative" are unfalsifiable, so readers discount them. Specifics are believable because they are checkable:

- A number with a bound: "cut checkout drop-off from 68% to 41%" is credible; "increased conversions by 300%" is not.
- A named client or a linked case study.
- A screenshot of the product instead of a stock photo of people smiling at a laptop.
- A testimonial that includes a limitation: "the migration took longer than planned, but nothing went down" reads truer than five stars.

Every claim on the site should have a proof next to it, or be removed. This is also the part that makes the writing work harder: you cannot fake it with tone.

## A concrete example of the same claim, three ways

Take the promise "deploy on Friday without fear":

- **Hero:** "Ship on Friday. Sleep on Saturday." The promise, compressed.
- **Section:** the three checks the pipeline runs before deploy, with the actual command and output.
- **Case study:** the incident that motivated it, what changed, and the deploy that would have failed before.

Same claim, three depths. A visitor who only reads the hero gets the idea; one who is evaluating gets the mechanism; one who is buying gets the proof. That is what a narrative does on a website: it survives skimming.

## Consistency is the hard part

A narrative breaks in the places nobody reviews: the error message, the email confirmation, the cookie banner, the empty state of a dashboard. Those strings are where the brand either sounds like a person or like a different company.

My rule is that one person owns the voice and everyone else can propose changes. In practice that means a short list of decisions rather than a tone-of-voice document:

- Contractions yes, exclamation marks no.
- Second person, never "the user".
- Numbers with their unit and their source.
- Errors say what happened and what to do next, in that order.

The visual side has to make the same promises, which is why [graphic design for digital marketing](/en/blog/graphic-design-digital-marketing/) starts with a system rather than a mood board.

## Test it before you polish it

Three cheap tests catch most narrative problems:

1. **The five-second test.** Show the homepage to someone unfamiliar for five seconds, then ask what the company does and for whom. If the answer is vague, the promise is vague.
2. **The competitor swap.** Replace your name with a competitor's in the hero. If it still reads true, rewrite it.
3. **The objection test.** Read the page as a sceptical buyer and write down every "but". Each unanswered "but" is a missing section.

Nothing here needs a workshop. It needs a sentence, a sequence and the discipline to delete what does not support them. If you want the technical half of the same page, the [technical SEO checklist](/en/blog/seo-optimization/) covers structure and metadata.
